Life, 1947–88

बम्बई Bambaī, Bombay, the city of his birth and of Saleem Sinai’s childhood, renamed Mumbai in 1995

Ahmed Salman Rushdie was born on 19 June 1947 in Bombay into a prosperous, liberal Muslim family, barely two months before India’s independence. His life is bound up with all three countries this seminar is about: India, Pakistan and Britain.

Click a card to turn it over.

Breakthrough: Midnight’s Children, 1981
  • Midnight’s Children won the Booker Prize in 1981. In 1993 it was named the “Booker of Bookers”, the best novel among the first 25 winners, and in 2008 it won the public vote for the “Best of the Booker”.
  • The novel made Rushdie the leading voice of a new Anglophone literature from the former colonies, and established magic realism, digressive storytelling and a hybrid English as his trademarks.
Shame, 1983, and a growing political profile
  • Shame, his novel about Pakistan, was shortlisted for the Booker Prize in 1983.
  • In The Jaguar Smile (1987) he wrote about a journey through Sandinista Nicaragua; in essays and journalism he increasingly took positions on racism in Britain and on politics in South Asia.

The two seminar novels

कहानी kahānī, Hindi and Urdu for “story”

The two books are often read as a pair: Midnight’s Children as Rushdie’s novel of India, Shame as his novel of Pakistan. Both turn the birth of a nation into the story of a family.

On the other pages The history behind Midnight’s Children is on the India page; Bhutto, Zia-ul-Haq and Benazir Bhutto, the models for Shame, are on the Pakistan page.

The fatwa

فتویٰ fatwā, a legal opinion issued by an Islamic religious authority

In September 1988 Rushdie published The Satanic Verses. Parts of the novel, which include dream sequences about a prophet in an imagined Arabian city, were condemned by many Muslims as blasphemous.

Bans, protests and a public debate
  • India banned the import of the book within weeks of its publication; many other countries with large Muslim populations followed. Protesters died in riots in Pakistan and India.
  • The affair became a defining debate about freedom of expression, religious offence and multiculturalism in Britain and beyond, and it still shapes how Rushdie is perceived.
Slowly back into the open, 1998–2022
  • In 1998 the Iranian government declared that it would neither support nor hinder the fatwa, although it was never formally revoked. Rushdie gradually resumed public life and moved to New York in 2000.
  • He was knighted in 2007, which provoked new protests in Pakistan and Iran.

The attack, 2022

چاقو chāqū, Urdu for “knife”

On 12 August 2022, more than 33 years after the fatwa, Rushdie was attacked on stage at the Chautauqua Institution in western New York, just before he was to give a talk. He was stabbed many times and survived, but lost the sight of his right eye.

Knife, 2024
  • In April 2024 Rushdie published Knife: Meditations After an Attempted Murder, an account of the attack, his recovery and his marriage to the poet Rachel Eliza Griffiths. In it he sets language against violence: language, too, he writes, is a knife.
The trials, 2025–26
  • In February 2025 a jury in Mayville, New York, found the attacker, Hadi Matar, guilty of attempted murder and assault; in May 2025 he was sentenced to 25 years in prison.
  • In July 2026 a federal jury also convicted him of terrorism offences, including attempting to provide material support to Hezbollah. Sentencing in the federal case is scheduled for November 2026.
Writing on
  • His first fiction after the attack, The Eleventh Hour (November 2025), is a book of five linked stories and novellas set in India, England and the United States.
