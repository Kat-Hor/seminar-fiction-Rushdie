Topics for presentations and essays

Topics are assigned on 5 November. The questions under each topic are not a checklist and have no answers to look up. They are starting points: take the ones that interest you, argue with them, and add your own.

S1The Historical Background and its Transformation in Shame

Questions to think with

  1. Rushdie does not name Bhutto and Zia but turns them into Iskander Harappa and Raza Hyder. What does the novel gain by this disguise, and what does it risk?
  2. At which points does turning history into fiction become a political act in itself? Would a straightforward history of Pakistan have been more or less subversive?

Background: Pakistan, Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, Zia-ul-Haq and Benazir Bhutto

S2Shame, Literature and its Relation to Reality

Questions to think with

  1. The narrator keeps breaking off the story to comment on real events in Pakistan and Britain. Why might a novel have to interrupt its own fiction in order to be taken seriously about reality?
  2. Can a novel tell a “truer” story about a country than journalism or historiography? Take a position and test it against a book, film or series that shaped your own picture of a country.
  3. The narrator says his country exists only “at a slight angle to reality”. What is gained, and what is evaded, by that small angle?
S3The Function of Magic Realism in Shame, or: Mediated Reality as Fantasy?

Questions to think with

  1. Choose one fantastic element, for instance Sufiya Zinobia’s burning blushes or her transformation into the Beast. Which reality does it make visible that realistic description could not?
  2. Is the fantastic in Shame an escape from reality or a sharper lens on it? Where exactly would you draw the line between the two?
S4Narrative Structure, Metanarrative and the Role of the “Author” in Shame

Questions to think with

  1. How far can you trust a narrator who keeps reminding you that he is inventing? Does his openness make the story more credible to you, or less?
  2. Why might Rushdie blur the line between himself and his narrator? What happens to your reading when author, narrator and character can no longer be kept apart?
S5The Concept/Metaphor of Shame

Questions to think with

  1. The novel ties shame closely to its opposite, shamelessness. Where do you see shame working as a means of social control in your own surroundings, and who is expected to feel it on behalf of others?
  2. Sufiya Zinobia absorbs the shame that others refuse to feel. Is shame in this novel a private emotion or a political force? What would follow for a society if it were the latter?
S6The Center and the Periphery, or: Where is the Hero?

Questions to think with

  1. Omar Khayyam Shakil is called a peripheral hero, standing at the margin of his own story. Why build a novel around such a figure, and what does it suggest about who makes history?
  2. Who is the real centre of Shame: Omar, Sufiya Zinobia, the narrator, or Pakistan itself? Argue for one and take the strongest objection seriously.
S7The Representation of Women in Shame

Questions to think with

  1. The narrator admits that the women seem to have taken over his story. Are the women in Shame victims, agents, or both? Compare two figures to find out.
  2. Does the novel critique patriarchal violence, or does it at times reproduce its images of women? Where do you feel that tension most strongly as a reader?
S8Reading Shame as a Postcolonial Novel?

Questions to think with

  1. What would be lost if we read Shame only as a postcolonial novel, and what would be lost if we ignored that frame altogether?
  2. The narrator writes in English, from London, about a country he has left. Who has the right to tell a nation’s story? Consider your own position as a reader in Germany: what do you bring to this novel, and what can you not see?
M1Language, or: The Magic Radio in Midnight’s Children

Questions to think with

  1. Saleem’s telepathy works like a radio that picks up the voices of a whole nation. What does this image suggest about the possibility, or impossibility, of one voice speaking for India?
  2. Rushdie mixes English with Hindi and Urdu words and the rhythms of Bombay speech. How did this English feel to you as a reader? What does it do to English as the language of the former colonizer?
M2Women in Midnight’s Children

Questions to think with

  1. A novel about the birth of a nation, told by a man, is full of mothers: Reverend Mother, Amina, Mary Pereira, the Widow. Which of these women holds real power over Saleem’s story, and what kind of power is it?
  2. Padma listens, doubts and interrupts. How would the novel change without her, and what does her role tell you about who controls a story?
M3Hybridity and Anglo-Indians/Minority Identities in Midnight’s Children

Questions to think with

  1. Saleem turns out to be the son of an Englishman and a Hindu woman, raised in a Muslim family. If blood does not make him who he is, what does? Ask the same question about your own sense of belonging.
  2. Which minorities are visible in the novel, and which remain in the background? What does that tell you about whose India is being narrated?
M4Origins and Family Relations in Midnight’s Children

Questions to think with

  1. Saleem has several fathers and several mothers. Why does the novel make origins so unstable, and is that a loss or a liberation?
  2. Think of your own family history as it is told at home. Which parts are facts and which are legends, and does the difference matter to the people who tell it?
M5Midnight’s Children and Magic Realism

Questions to think with

  1. The 1,001 children born in the first hour of independence all have magical gifts. Why might a novel about India’s history need the impossible in order to be truthful?
  2. Compare one magical moment in Midnight’s Children with one in Shame. Is magic realism doing the same work in both novels, or does it change with the country being written about?
M6(Re-)Writing Indian History

Questions to think with

  1. Saleem notices that he has put Gandhi’s assassination on the wrong date, and leaves the error in. Why would a novel about history keep a mistake on purpose?
  2. Saleem believes he is responsible for the events of national history. Is this megalomania, satire, or a serious claim about how every individual is entangled in history? Where would you place yourself in the history of your own time?
M7The Partition of India and the Perforated Sheet

Questions to think with

  1. Aadam Aziz gets to know his future wife only in fragments, through a hole in a sheet. How does this way of seeing relate to the partition of a subcontinent?
  2. Can a country, or a person, ever be seen whole? What does the novel suggest, and do you agree?
  3. Think of a place or a person you only know in pieces, from the news, from social media or from family stories. Which picture have you assembled, and what might be missing?
M8Storytelling and Fantasy / The Importance of Narratives

Questions to think with

  1. Saleem compares his writing to making pickles and chutneys. What does it mean to preserve history like this, with flavour, distortion and selection, and is any other kind of preserving possible?
  2. Which stories hold your family, your town or your country together? What happens when competing stories collide, as they did in 1947?

Each topic is the basis for an oral presentation and a written essay.
