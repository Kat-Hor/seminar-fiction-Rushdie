The founding of Pakistan

پاک pāk, Urdu for “pure”: Pakistan is often rendered as “land of the pure”

Pakistan was brought into being at the time of the Partition of British India in 1947 in order to create a separate homeland for India’s Muslims, in response to the demands of Islamic nationalists articulated by the All India Muslim League under the leadership of Mohammed Ali Jinnah.

Click a card to turn it over.

  • The gradual clarification of the British intention to grant self-government to India along the lines of British parliamentary democracy aroused Muslim apprehensions regarding ultimate political subjection to the Hindu majority.
  • British policy, supported by the weight of the Hindu nationalist movement, laboured hard to avoid disrupting the economic and political unity built up during British rule. None of the suggested alternatives to the separation of Pakistan commended themselves to Jinnah. His courage and implacable determination triumphed in the end.
A state that seemed unlikely to survive
  • With the major portion of the wealth and resources of the British heritage passing to India, Pakistan’s survival seemed to hang in the balance. Of all the well-organized provinces of British India, only comparatively backward areas came to Pakistan intact. The Punjab and Bengal were divided, and Kashmir became disputed territory.
  • Economically, the situation seemed almost hopeless: the new frontier cut off Pakistani raw materials from the Indian factories, disrupting industry, commerce, and agriculture.
  • The partition and the movement of refugees were accompanied by terrible massacres for which both communities were responsible. India remained openly unfriendly; its economic superiority expressed itself in a virtual blockade. The dispute over Kashmir brought the two countries to the verge of war.
In Rushdie The narrator of Shame reminds his readers that the name Pakistan was thought up in England by Muslim intellectuals, as an acronym of the regions it was meant to unite. Pakistan, he suggests, may simply have been “insufficiently imagined”.

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto

روٹی، کپڑا اور مکان roṭī, kapṛā aur makān, “bread, clothing and shelter”, the slogan of Bhutto’s Pakistan People’s Party

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto (born 5 January 1928 near Larkana, Sindh; died 4 April 1979 in Rawalpindi) served as president (1971–73) and prime minister (1973–77) of Pakistan. Although a popular leader, he was overthrown and executed by the military.

Minister under Ayub Khan, 1958–66
  • In Karachi he set up a law practice and was appointed a member of Pakistan’s delegation to the United Nations in 1957.
  • After Mohammad Ayub Khan seized the government in 1958, Bhutto was appointed commerce minister and then held other cabinet posts. As foreign minister (1963–66) he began working for greater independence from Western powers and for closer ties with China.
The 1970 elections and the loss of East Pakistan
  • After the overthrow of the Ayub Khan regime by Gen. Agha Muhammad Yahya Khan, national elections were held in 1970. Although Bhutto and his party won a sweeping electoral victory in West Pakistan, the biggest election winner was the Awami League, an East Pakistan-based party that had campaigned for full autonomy for East Pakistan.
  • Bhutto refused to form a government with this separatist party, causing a nullification of the election. The widespread rioting that followed degenerated into civil war, after which East Pakistan, with the help of India, emerged as the independent state of Bangladesh.
President and prime minister, 1971–77
  • After West Pakistan’s humiliating defeat by India, Yahya Khan turned the government over to Bhutto on 20 December 1971. Bhutto placed his predecessor under house arrest, nationalized several key industries, and undertook the taxation of the landed families in his first acts as president.
  • After the new constitution (1973) made the presidency largely ceremonial, Bhutto became prime minister. In both capacities he also filled the cabinet posts of foreign affairs, defence, and interior. His government, retaining martial law, began a process of Islamization.
Fall and execution, 1977–79
  • Sensing that the public was turning against his rule by decree, Bhutto ordered new elections in 1977 to obtain a popular mandate. His party won by a large majority, but the opposition charged him with electoral fraud.
  • The government was seized by Gen. Mohammad Zia-ul-Haq, the army chief of staff, on 5 July 1977. Soon afterward Bhutto was imprisoned. He was sentenced to death (18 March 1978) on the charge of having ordered the assassination of a political opponent in 1974; after an appeal to a higher court, he was hanged on 4 April 1979, despite appeals for clemency from several world leaders.
  • His daughter, Benazir Bhutto, later became Pakistan’s first female prime minister; his grandson, Bilawal Bhutto Zardari, took on the leadership of the PPP in 2007.
In Rushdie In Shame, Iskander Harappa, the playboy turned charismatic politician, is widely read as a portrait of Zulfikar Ali Bhutto. Like Bhutto, he is overthrown by his own army chief and hanged.

Zia-ul-Haq

نظامِ مصطفیٰ niẓām-e muṣṭafā, “the system of the Prophet”, the slogan of the Islamization of state and society

Mohammad Zia-ul-Haq (born 12 August 1924 in Jullundur, Punjab, now in India; died 17 August 1988 near Bahawalpur) was chief of army staff, chief martial-law administrator, and president of Pakistan (1978–88).

Eliminating Bhutto
  • Zia’s change of direction hinted that there were powers behind the scene determined to eliminate Bhutto as an active player. The decision was made not to conduct new elections, to arrest and try Bhutto, and ultimately to ignore the pleadings from other governments to spare his life. Found guilty and sentenced to death, Bhutto was hanged on 4 April 1979.
  • Zia assumed the presidency after Fazal Elahi Chaudhry resigned in 1978. He suspended political parties in 1979, banned labour strikes, imposed strict censorship on the press, and declared martial law in the country (nominally lifted in 1985).
Islamization
  • A devout Muslim, Zia believed that religious tradition should guide Pakistan’s institutions in all aspects of daily life. Islamization became the guiding principle of his plan to reform Pakistan, to reassure its unity, and to galvanize the country to meet all threats, foreign and domestic. Clearly, the programme was also geared to reinforce his rule and establish his legitimacy.
  • The 1979 revolution in Iran, which ended the Pahlavi monarchy, dovetailed with developments in Pakistan. Zia called for criminal punishments in keeping with Islamic law and insisted on banking practices and economic activity that followed Islamic experience.
  • In 1984 he put his Islamization programme to a referendum and coupled it to a vote of confidence in his presidency that would give him another five years in office. He won overwhelming approval, though only half of the eligible voters took part and the opposition insisted that the vote was rigged.
Afghanistan: Pakistan as a “frontline state”
  • The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 reinforced Zia’s belief that only Islamic practices could unite the Muslims of Pakistan and Afghanistan against an alien and aggressive neighbour. Zia played a major role in assisting the Afghan resistance, the mujahideen, whose leaders set up their headquarters in and around Peshawar. Several million Afghan refugees came to Pakistan, most of them housed in camps near the border.
  • Pakistan sought help from other Muslim states, especially Saudi Arabia, and after 1981 from the United States under Ronald Reagan. Pakistan became the third largest recipient of U.S. military aid, several billion dollars by the end of Reagan’s second term, even though Islamabad was known to be pursuing an aggressive nuclear weapons programme. U.S. intelligence services did not discourage Pakistan’s Inter-Service Intelligence from working closely with the most radical religious movements in Afghanistan.
Elections without parties and the end of martial law, 1985–86
  • In February 1985 Zia allowed national and provincial assembly elections, though without the participation of political parties. His opponents called his Islamic system a ploy to acquire still wider powers and urged a boycott, but voters turned out in considerable numbers. Zia handpicked Muhammad Khan Junejo, a Sindhi politician and landowner, as prime minister.
  • Martial law was officially lifted in December 1985. Junejo revived the Pakistan Muslim League, the Muslim League (J), and Benazir Bhutto returned from exile to lead the PPP. The key rivals for national power were now the Muslim League (J) and Bhutto’s PPP.
Ethnic violence in Karachi
  • Lifting martial law coincided with intensified conflict between Pakistan’s ethnic communities, above all in Karachi. Native Sindhis complained that the muhajirs, Muslim immigrants from India, monopolized political and economic power in the city. The founding of the Muhajir Qaumi Movement (MQM) in the mid-1980s was both a cause and a consequence of the violence against the immigrant community.
  • Pashtun migrants, notably Afghan Pashtuns, moving from the frontier region to Karachi posed a further challenge, and a narcotics and weapons trade rooted in the North-West Frontier Province fuelled the conflict. By 1986 intercommunal violence in Karachi had reached a level not seen since partition, with riots also in Quetta and Hyderabad, and the army was called in to restore order.
The last year, 1988
  • Confronting major opposition, intensified ethnic warfare and a troubled economy, Zia dissolved the national and provincial assemblies in May 1988 and dismissed the Junejo government, alleging corruption and weakness. He promised another national election and in June made himself head of a caretaker government. Although the country was in considerable disarray, Zia pretended that everything was under control, until his death in the plane crash of 17 August 1988.
In Rushdie In Shame, General Raza Hyder, who overthrows Iskander Harappa and governs in the name of God, is modelled on Zia-ul-Haq.

Benazir Bhutto

بے نظیر be-naẓīr, “without equal, incomparable”: the meaning of her first name

Benazir Bhutto (born 21 June 1953 in Karachi; died 27 December 2007 in Rawalpindi) became the first woman leader of a Muslim nation in modern history. She served two terms as prime minister of Pakistan, 1988–90 and 1993–96, and was assassinated while campaigning for parliamentary elections.

Political beginnings, 1977–88
  • Shortly after she completed her studies in 1977 and returned to Pakistan, her father was deposed by Gen. Zia-ul-Haq. After her father’s execution in 1979, she became the titular head of the Pakistan People’s Party (PPP) and endured frequent house arrest from 1979 to 1984.
  • In exile from 1984 to 1986, she returned after the lifting of martial law and soon became the foremost figure of the opposition to Zia. After Zia’s death in August 1988, the PPP won the single largest bloc of seats in the National Assembly.
First term, 1988–90
  • She formed a fragile coalition with independent parliamentarians from her home province of Sindh, but they left the following year as ethnic tensions in the province escalated. Without them she was unable to pass legislation to address poverty, governmental corruption and increasing crime, and she bore the brunt of a discordant relationship with the military leadership.
  • In August 1990 President Ghulam Ishaq Khan dismissed her government on charges of corruption and other malfeasance. After the PPP’s defeat in the elections of October 1990, she led the opposition against her successor, Nawaz Sharif.
Second term, 1993–96
  • In October 1993 the PPP won a plurality of votes and beat Sharif’s Pakistan Muslim League (PML-N) in every province except Balochistan, including Sharif’s home province of Punjab. Bhutto made headway in foreign relations, attracted foreign investment and implemented social programmes, with an ally in President Farooq Leghari, a member of the PPP.
  • But the economy remained unstable and law and order declined. Her brother Murtaza accused her husband, Asif Ali Zardari, of corruption. With her public support dwindling, Leghari dismissed her government in November 1996.
Corruption charges and exile, 1997–2007
  • After the PPP’s decisive defeat in 1997, Sharif’s administration pursued the corruption charges against her with British and Swiss cooperation. In 1999 she and her husband were convicted by a Lahore court, a verdict overturned by the Supreme Court in 2001 because of evidence of governmental interference.
  • She reached no accommodation with Gen. Pervez Musharraf, who seized power in a coup in 1999. Facing arrest warrants, she remained in exile in London and Dubai. A 2002 decree barred prime ministers from a third term, and a law of 2000 kept convicted individuals from party office; the PPP therefore registered a legally separate branch, the Pakistan People’s Party Parliamentarians (PPPP), which won a strong vote in 2002.
Return and assassination, 2007
  • Shortly before his re-election, Musharraf granted her an amnesty for the corruption charges, which the Supreme Court criticized as unconstitutional. In October 2007 she returned to Karachi after eight years of self-imposed exile; the celebrations were marred by a suicide attack on her motorcade that killed numerous supporters.
  • In December 2007 she was assassinated in a similar attack while campaigning for the upcoming parliamentary elections.
In Rushdie Iskander Harappa’s daughter Arjumand, the “virgin Ironpants” who guards her father’s legacy, has often been read as an allusion to Benazir Bhutto. Shame appeared in 1983, during Benazir’s years under house arrest and five years before she became prime minister.

Timeline

  1. 1940The Muslim League under Jinnah formally endorses the demand for Pakistan.
  2. August 1947Partition of British India; Pakistan is founded in two parts, West and East.
  3. 1958Ayub Khan seizes power; Zulfikar Ali Bhutto becomes commerce minister.
  4. 1963–66Bhutto is foreign minister; he resigns after the peace with India that follows the 1965 war over Kashmir.
  5. December 1967Bhutto founds the Pakistan People’s Party.
  6. 1970National elections: the Awami League wins overall, the PPP in West Pakistan.
  7. December 1971East Pakistan becomes Bangladesh; on 20 December Yahya Khan hands the government to Bhutto.
  8. 1973New constitution; Bhutto becomes prime minister.
  9. 5 July 1977Zia-ul-Haq seizes power in a bloodless coup.
  10. 4 April 1979Bhutto is hanged in Rawalpindi.
  11. December 1979Soviet invasion of Afghanistan; Pakistan becomes a “frontline state”.
  12. 1983Rushdie’s Shame is published.
  13. 1984Referendum on Islamization and on five more years for Zia.
  14. 1985–86Elections without parties; martial law lifted; Benazir Bhutto returns from exile.
  15. 17 August 1988Zia dies in a plane crash near Bahawalpur.
  16. 1 December 1988Benazir Bhutto becomes prime minister (dismissed 1990; second term 1993–96).
  17. 1999Pervez Musharraf seizes power; Benazir Bhutto lives in exile.
  18. 27 Dec 2007Benazir Bhutto is assassinated while campaigning.
